🔴Cellulitis and Cutaneous Abscess

Pathophysiologic Basis
  • Cellulitis is a spreading infection of dermis/subcutaneous tissue, classically associated with β-hemolytic streptococci and Staphylococcus aureus; antibacterial therapy must achieve active concentrations in infected soft tissue.
  • β-Lactams kill susceptible streptococci/MSSA by binding penicillin-binding proteins and inhibiting transpeptidation/peptidoglycan cross-linking; their killing is principally time-dependent.
  • MRSA commonly expresses an altered penicillin-binding protein, making many ordinary β-lactams ineffective; vancomycin bypasses PBPs and instead interferes directly with cell-wall precursor processing. Katzung describes vancomycin as a bactericidal cell-wall inhibitor and a standard parenteral agent against methicillin-resistant staphylococci.
  • A cutaneous abscess is a high-inoculum purulent cavity; SUPPLEMENTARY incision and drainage corrects the key mechanical lesion and is pharmacologically more important than simply escalating systemic antibiotics.
  • Purulence, systemic illness, immunocompromise, recurrent infection, rapid spread, or severe local disease increases the need for systemic MRSA-active therapy SUPPLEMENTARY.
Clinical Features and Diagnostic Strategy
  • Nonpurulent cellulitis typically causes diffuse erythema, warmth, edema and tenderness without a discrete fluctuant collection.
  • Fluctuance, focal tenderness and purulent drainage suggest abscess; SUPPLEMENTARY bedside ultrasound may identify an occult collection when examination is equivocal.
  • Rapid progression, bullae, crepitus, skin necrosis, hypotension, severe pain out of proportion or systemic toxicity should trigger evaluation for necrotizing soft-tissue infection, not simple cellulitis.
  • Culture is most useful from purulent drainage, severe disease, recurrent infection or unusual exposures; uncomplicated nonpurulent cellulitis often has no useful culture target.
  • Review renal function, β-lactam allergy, previous MRSA, recent antimicrobial therapy, pregnancy and immune status before selecting treatment.

💀Drug-Induced Cutaneous Disorders

Pathophysiologic Basis
  • Immediate drug hypersensitivity may be IgE-mediated type I disease with mast-cell degranulation, histamine/leukotriene release, vasodilation, vascular leak and bronchoconstriction.
  • Delayed drug eruptions frequently involve T-cell–mediated mechanisms; SJS/TEN SUPPLEMENTARY represent severe cytotoxic epidermal injury with widespread keratinocyte apoptosis.
  • Katzung identifies sulfonamides as classic causes of skin rash, exfoliative dermatitis and rare but potentially fatal Stevens–Johnson syndrome.
  • Other classic pharmacologic culprits SUPPLEMENTARY include aromatic antiseizure agents, allopurinol, selected NSAIDs and several antimicrobials.
  • The initiating lesion in severe drug reactions is continuing antigenic drug exposure; therefore the single most important first pharmacologic action is immediate withdrawal of the culprit agent.
Clinical Features and Diagnostic Strategy
  • Simple exanthematous hypersensitivity commonly causes symmetric morbilliform/maculopapular rash without extensive mucosal injury or skin detachment.
  • Urticaria, angioedema, wheeze, hypotension or airway compromise indicate immediate hypersensitivity; hypotension/bronchospasm/angioedema constitute anaphylaxis.
  • SUPPLEMENTARY SJS/TEN warning features: fever, painful dusky or targetoid skin, mucosal erosions, blistering, positive epidermal detachment and systemic involvement.
  • SUPPLEMENTARY Classification by epidermal detachment: SJS <10% body-surface area, SJS/TEN overlap 10–30%, TEN >30%.
  • Review all recently introduced drugs and stop nonessential high-risk medications; the correct pharmacologic diagnosis often depends more on temporal exposure than on serum drug levels.
Pharmacologic Management
  • Epinephrine — sympathomimetic: LIFE-SAVING first-line for anaphylaxis; α1 activation raises SVR and decreases mucosal edema, β1 increases cardiac output, and β2 bronchodilates and reduces mediator release. Katzung specifically calls epinephrine the drug of choice for immediate anaphylactic shock and states antihistamines/steroids act more slowly and are less effective.
  • H1 antihistamine — Diphenhydramine/Cetirizine: competitive/inverse H1 receptor antagonism reduces urticaria and pruritus but does not reverse shock or life-threatening airway obstruction.
  • Glucocorticoid — Methylprednisolone/Prednisone [SUPPLEMENTARY acute placement]: intracellular GR activation alters inflammatory transcription; onset is delayed, so glucocorticoids are adjunctive rather than epinephrine substitutes.
  • SJS/TEN SUPPLEMENTARY: immediate culprit-drug withdrawal + burn/ICU-level supportive care, fluid/electrolyte replacement, wound care, pain control, temperature control and ophthalmologic/mucosal management.
  • SJS/TEN immunomodulation SUPPLEMENTARY: cyclosporine, systemic glucocorticoids, etanercept or IVIG have been used in selected settings, but the supplied pharmacology source does not establish one universally proven antidote or standard pharmacologic rescue regimen.
Therapeutic Pitfalls, Contraindications, and Monitoring
  • CONTRAINDICATED: treating anaphylaxis with antihistamine alone; delayed epinephrine can be fatal.
  • CONTRAINDICATED: rechallenge with the culprit drug after SJS/TEN except under extraordinary specialist circumstances; recurrence may be rapid and severe SUPPLEMENTARY.
  • Sulfonamides may additionally cause hematologic toxicity, crystalluria and G6PD-associated hemolysis, complicating severe cutaneous reactions.
  • [SUPPLEMENTARY pharmacogenomic associations]: carbamazepine–HLA-B15:02 and allopurinol–HLA-B58:01 are classic severe-cutaneous-reaction pairings in susceptible populations.
  • Monitor airway/BP/O₂ saturation in anaphylaxis; in SJS/TEN monitor skin-detachment extent, fluids, electrolytes, renal/hepatic function, infection and ocular/genitourinary mucosal complications.

🤕Pain Management

Pathophysiologic Basis
  • Inflammatory nociception is amplified by prostaglandins that sensitize peripheral pain fibers; NSAIDs reduce pain by inhibiting cyclooxygenase and prostaglandin synthesis.
  • Acetaminophen is an effective analgesic/antipyretic with weak peripheral anti-inflammatory activity, making it better suited to pain/fever than to strong inflammatory suppression.
  • Dμ-Opioid receptor agonists couple to Gi/o proteins → ↓adenylyl cyclase/cAMP, open postsynaptic K⁺ channels and close presynaptic Ca²⁺ channels → reduced neurotransmitter release and pain transmission.
  • Katzung identifies morphine as the prototypic strong opioid and naloxone as a strong μ-receptor antagonist.
  • SUPPLEMENTARY Neuropathic pain may respond better to α2δ calcium-channel ligands, SNRIs or selected TCAs than to escalating NSAIDs or opioids.
Clinical Features and Diagnostic Strategy
  • Classify pain as inflammatory, mechanical, neuropathic, visceral, acute postoperative/traumatic, malignant or chronic before choosing the pharmacologic target.
  • Assess renal function, GI-bleed risk, cardiovascular disease and anticoagulant use before NSAIDs.
  • Assess liver disease, alcohol use and cumulative combination-product exposure before acetaminophen.
  • Assess respiratory disease, frailty, renal/hepatic function and concomitant sedatives before opioids.
  • Pain relief should be accompanied by improved function; progressive sedation is toxicity, not therapeutic success.

🦴Osteoporosis

Pathophysiologic Basis
  • Osteoporosis reflects loss of bone strength from imbalance between osteoclast-mediated resorption and osteoblast-mediated formation.
  • Bisphosphonates bind bone mineral and inhibit osteoclast activity, directly reducing excessive skeletal resorption.
  • Denosumab neutralizes RANKL, preventing RANK-mediated osteoclast differentiation and activity.
  • Teriparatide exploits the pharmacodynamic difference between continuous PTH exposure—which favors resorption—and intermittent PTH exposure—which stimulates net bone formation.
  • Estrogen/SERM effects decrease postmenopausal resorption; chronic systemic glucocorticoids do the opposite and are a major pharmacologic cause of osteoporosis.
Clinical Features and Diagnostic Strategy
  • Assess fragility fracture history, DEXA bone-mineral density, age, menopause/hypogonadism, chronic glucocorticoid use, falls, calcium/vitamin-D intake and renal function.
  • Correct clinically significant hypocalcemia and vitamin-D deficiency before potent antiresorptive therapy.
  • Significant esophageal disease or inability to remain upright makes oral bisphosphonate therapy unsuitable.
  • Severe renal dysfunction changes bisphosphonate choice/dosing and increases hypocalcemia risk with denosumab.
  • Treatment success is measured by fracture prevention, BMD trajectory, adherence and minimization of therapy-specific toxicity.

✋Rheumatoid Arthritis

Pathophysiologic Basis
  • RA is autoimmune inflammatory synovitis involving T cells, B cells, macrophages and cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6, with progressive cartilage/bone destruction.
  • NSAIDs reduce prostaglandin-mediated pain and stiffness but do not modify the underlying immune disease or prevent structural damage.
  • Katzung defines DMARDs by their ability to slow or sometimes reverse joint damage, an effect not observed with NSAIDs.
  • Methotrexate reduces immune-cell/inflammatory activity and functions as the classic anchor conventional DMARD.
  • Biologics/targeted therapies interrupt specific pathways—TNF, IL-6, B cells, T-cell costimulation, JAK signaling—rather than providing nonspecific symptomatic analgesia.
Clinical Features and Diagnostic Strategy
  • Typical RA produces symmetric inflammatory polyarthritis, prolonged morning stiffness, synovial swelling and progressive deformity.
  • Assess inflammatory activity, functional impairment, erosions, RF/anti-CCP, CBC, renal/hepatic function and extra-articular disease.
  • Before major immunosuppression, evaluate latent TB, hepatitis risk, vaccinations and active infection SUPPLEMENTARY.
  • Distinguish active RA from septic arthritis, gout, OA and fibromyalgia before escalating immunosuppression.
  • Treatment should be adjusted when clinical/radiographic progression continues despite apparent symptom control.

🦴Osteoarthritis

Pathophysiologic Basis
  • OA is primarily degenerative articular cartilage loss with subchondral remodeling and variable secondary synovial inflammation.
  • Pharmacologic treatment reduces pain and inflammatory sensitization but does not regenerate cartilage or reliably halt structural degeneration.
  • NSAIDs reduce prostaglandin-mediated pain arising from secondary synovial inflammation.
  • Acetaminophen provides analgesia without substantial peripheral anti-inflammatory action.
  • SUPPLEMENTARY Topical delivery can produce high local NSAID exposure while reducing systemic exposure in localized disease.
Clinical Features and Diagnostic Strategy
  • OA causes activity-related pain, brief morning stiffness, crepitus, bony enlargement and decreased range of motion.
  • Assess severity and function before selecting pharmacotherapy; exercise, weight management and mechanical interventions remain central SUPPLEMENTARY.
  • Exclude RA, septic arthritis, gout or fracture when a joint is markedly inflamed, acutely swollen or systemically symptomatic.
  • Assess GI bleeding, renal disease, cardiovascular disease, anticoagulation and hypertension before chronic oral NSAIDs.
  • Treatment endpoint is improved pain and function rather than suppression of inflammatory biomarkers.
Pharmacologic Management
  • DNSAID — Ibuprofen/Naproxen/Diclofenac: reversible COX inhibition → ↓prostaglandin-mediated pain; source-supported toxicities include GI ulcer/bleeding, renal injury and fluid/BP effects.
  • COX-2 selective NSAID — Celecoxib: retains analgesic/anti-inflammatory activity with less platelet COX-1 inhibition but does not eliminate renal or cardiovascular toxicity.
  • Acetaminophen: analgesic/antipyretic option when inflammatory disease is limited or NSAID risk is high; efficacy may be more modest in OA SUPPLEMENTARY.
  • Topical diclofenac SUPPLEMENTARY: useful for localized knee/hand disease when systemic NSAID exposure is undesirable.
  • Intra-articular glucocorticoid SUPPLEMENTARY: short-term relief for selected symptomatic joints; not structural disease modification.
  • Duloxetine SUPPLEMENTARY: SNRI option for selected chronic OA pain, particularly when central pain amplification contributes.
Therapeutic Pitfalls, Contraindications, and Monitoring
  • Chronic NSAIDs can worsen hypertension, edema, HF, peptic-ulcer disease and CKD; use the lowest effective exposure.
  • COX-2 selectivity is not equivalent to cardiovascular safety; Katzung specifically notes increased thrombotic events with highly selective COX-2 inhibition.
  • Acetaminophen has less GI/platelet toxicity but overdose produces severe hepatic necrosis; cumulative combination products must be counted.
  • Frequent intra-articular glucocorticoid injection should not substitute for reassessment of severe progressive structural disease SUPPLEMENTARY.
  • Monitor pain/function, BP, creatinine, GI bleeding symptoms and liver exposure when acetaminophen is used chronically.

🦠Septic Arthritis

Pathophysiologic Basis
  • Septic arthritis is bacterial infection of the synovial space; bacterial proliferation plus neutrophilic inflammation can rapidly destroy cartilage.
  • S. aureus is a major pathogen; Gram-negative bacilli and N. gonorrhoeae become important according to age and exposure.
  • Antimicrobial therapy kills organisms, while SUPPLEMENTARY joint drainage reduces pus, bacterial inoculum and destructive inflammatory pressure.
  • Vancomycin covers MRSA; β-lactams such as ceftriaxone/cefazolin provide appropriate Gram-negative, gonococcal or MSSA activity depending context.
  • Delayed antimicrobial/source-control therapy can result in irreversible joint destruction.
Clinical Features and Diagnostic Strategy
  • Acute monoarthritis with pain, warmth, marked limitation of motion and fever should be treated as septic arthritis until excluded.
  • LIFE-SAVING diagnostic step SUPPLEMENTARY: arthrocentesis for cell count, Gram stain, culture and crystals before antibiotics when feasible without dangerous delay.
  • The presence of urate crystals does not exclude simultaneous infection SUPPLEMENTARY.
  • Blood cultures should be obtained in febrile/systemically ill patients.
  • Prosthetic joint, recent surgery, injection-drug use, gonococcal exposure and immunosuppression change organism probabilities and regimen selection.
Pharmacologic Management
  • Vancomycin: D-Ala-D-Ala binding inhibits cell-wall synthesis; IV systemic therapy; renal elimination; MRSA-active.
  • Ceftriaxone [SUPPLEMENTARY disease placement]: third-generation cephalosporin, PBP inhibitor, used for Gram-negative or gonococcal coverage.
  • Cefazolin/Nafcillin/Oxacillin SUPPLEMENTARY: narrower definitive MSSA therapy after susceptibility confirms methicillin sensitivity.
  • [SUPPLEMENTARY empiric severe adult approach]: vancomycin plus appropriate Gram-negative coverage until culture data permit narrowing.
  • SUPPLEMENTARY Drainage: repeated aspiration, arthroscopy or operative washout depending joint/response.
  • Katzung’s antimicrobial pharmacology emphasizes that β-lactams and vancomycin demonstrate predominantly time-dependent killing, unlike concentration-dependent aminoglycosides/fluoroquinolones.
Therapeutic Pitfalls, Contraindications, and Monitoring
  • CONTRAINDICATED strategy: diagnosing acute hot monoarthritis as gout without synovial evaluation when septic arthritis is plausible.
  • Do not continue unnecessarily broad therapy after organism and susceptibility become known.
  • Vancomycin plus other nephrotoxins increases AKI risk; monitor renal function and therapeutic exposure.
  • Failure to improve despite susceptible antibiotics suggests inadequate drainage, osteomyelitis, prosthetic infection, abscess or incorrect diagnosis.
  • Monitor fever, pain/range of motion, cultures, inflammatory-marker trend, CBC, renal/hepatic toxicity and source control.

🫁Tuberculous Arthritis

Pathophysiologic Basis
  • TB arthritis is an indolent osteoarticular infection caused by slow-growing intracellular mycobacteria; monotherapy rapidly selects resistant organisms.
  • Katzung emphasizes that mycobacterial disease requires combination therapy because of slow growth, intracellular location, chronicity and resistance.
  • Isoniazid targets mycolic-acid synthesis; rifampin blocks DNA-dependent RNA polymerase; ethambutol disrupts arabinogalactan cell wall; pyrazinamide requires bioactivation and is particularly useful against selected intracellular populations.
  • Multidrug therapy attacks organisms in different metabolic compartments while delaying resistance.
  • SUPPLEMENTARY Bone/joint TB may require longer total treatment than uncomplicated pulmonary TB depending on guideline, response and disease extent.
Clinical Features and Diagnostic Strategy
  • TB arthritis usually presents as chronic monoarthritis with pain, swelling, restricted motion and less dramatic systemic inflammation than pyogenic septic arthritis.
  • Diagnosis requires mycobacterial culture/NAAT and often synovial tissue/biopsy rather than ordinary bacterial culture alone.
  • Assess other pulmonary/extrapulmonary TB sites and HIV/immunosuppression.
  • Obtain baseline hepatic and renal function plus visual acuity/color discrimination before multidrug treatment.
  • Perform a complete medication review because rifampin profoundly alters CYP/P-gp substrate exposure.

🦶Gouty Arthritis

Pathophysiologic Basis
  • Gout results from monosodium urate crystal deposition secondary to chronic hyperuricemia; acute attacks are driven by intense neutrophilic inflammation.
  • Acute drugs suppress inflammation but do not materially decrease the body urate pool.
  • Chronic urate-lowering drugs reduce urate production or increase excretion, thereby preventing new crystal deposition and gradually dissolving existing stores.
  • Katzung divides gout therapy into acute anti-inflammatory treatment with NSAIDs, glucocorticoids or colchicine, uricosuric therapy with probenecid, and xanthine-oxidase inhibition with allopurinol/febuxostat.
  • Early urate lowering can mobilize tissue urate and provoke flares; temporary anti-inflammatory prophylaxis is therefore often used SUPPLEMENTARY.
Clinical Features and Diagnostic Strategy
  • Acute gout produces abrupt severe monoarthritis, classically at the first MTP joint, with redness, swelling and exquisite tenderness.
  • [SUPPLEMENTARY definitive diagnosis]: needle-shaped, negatively birefringent monosodium urate crystals in synovial fluid.
  • Serum urate can be normal during an acute flare and should not be used alone to exclude gout.
  • Septic arthritis must remain in the differential, particularly with fever, immunosuppression or atypical joint involvement.
  • Determine renal function, ulcer/CV risk, interacting drugs and urate burden before selecting acute/chronic therapy.
Acute Gout
  • DNSAID — Indomethacin/Naproxen: COX inhibition → ↓prostaglandin-mediated joint inflammation; effective but limited by renal/GI/CV toxicity.
  • DColchicine: binds tubulin → prevents microtubule assembly → reduces neutrophil migration/phagocytosis and inflammatory amplification; Katzung notes high doses frequently cause severe diarrhea.
  • Glucocorticoid — Prednisone/Intra-articular steroid: GR agonism rapidly suppresses inflammatory transcription and is useful when NSAID/colchicine is unsuitable [SUPPLEMENTARY disease placement].
  • SUPPLEMENTARY IL-1 inhibitors may be used in selected refractory/contraindicated cases but are not the classic PLE prototype.
  • Critical distinction: colchicine, NSAIDs and glucocorticoids treat inflammation, not chronic hyperuricemia.

🦋Systemic Lupus Erythematosus / Connective-Tissue Autoimmunity

Pathophysiologic Basis
  • SLE is a systemic autoimmune disease involving B-cell autoantibody production, immune complexes, T-cell abnormalities and inflammatory organ injury.
  • Mild cutaneous/musculoskeletal disease requires less immunosuppression than nephritis, CNS disease, pulmonary hemorrhage or severe vasculitis.
  • Hydroxychloroquine [SUPPLEMENTARY disease placement] modifies lysosomal antigen processing/TLR signaling and reduces chronic autoimmune activation.
  • Glucocorticoids rapidly suppress broad inflammatory transcription; cytotoxic/antimetabolite agents provide steroid-sparing control in organ-threatening disease.
  • Katzung’s DMARD table specifically lists belimumab with systemic lupus erythematosus and includes hydroxychloroquine, methotrexate, cyclosporine and rituximab among immune-modifying agents.
Clinical Features and Diagnostic Strategy
  • Determine whether disease involves skin/joints alone or kidney, CNS, blood, lung or cardiovascular systems because pharmacologic intensity is organ-specific.
  • Before major immunosuppression, obtain CBC, renal/hepatic function, urinalysis/proteinuria assessment, pregnancy status, infection screening and vaccination review SUPPLEMENTARY.
  • Infection can mimic a lupus flare; escalating immunosuppression before excluding infection can be dangerous.
  • Renal biopsy frequently determines the appropriate intensity of therapy for lupus nephritis SUPPLEMENTARY.
  • Monitor both inflammatory disease activity and irreversible organ damage rather than treating a laboratory value in isolation.
Pharmacologic Management
  • Hydroxychloroquine SUPPLEMENTARY: lysosomal/TLR immunomodulation; major toxicity is retinopathy; generally a maintenance cornerstone for many SLE phenotypes.
  • DGlucocorticoid — Prednisone/Methylprednisolone: intracellular GR activation → altered inflammatory gene transcription; rapid efficacy but dose-dependent infection, osteoporosis, diabetes, cataract, myopathy and HPA suppression.
  • DMycophenolate mofetil SUPPLEMENTARY: converted to mycophenolic acid → inhibits IMPDH → preferentially suppresses lymphocyte guanine synthesis; toxicities include GI effects, marrow suppression, infection and teratogenicity.
  • Cyclophosphamide SUPPLEMENTARY: alkylating agent producing phosphoramide mustard and acrolein; severe organ-threatening autoimmune disease use is limited by marrow suppression, infertility, malignancy and hemorrhagic cystitis.
  • DAzathioprine SUPPLEMENTARY: converted to 6-mercaptopurine → purine-synthesis inhibition; TPMT/NUDT15 status affects marrow toxicity.
  • Belimumab: inhibits B-lymphocyte stimulator/BLyS; Katzung lists nausea, diarrhea and respiratory infection as important toxicities.
Therapeutic Pitfalls, Contraindications, and Monitoring
  • Hydroxychloroquine requires ophthalmologic screening based on cumulative exposure/dose risk.
  • DCyclophosphamide → acrolein hemorrhagic cystitis → MESNA + hydration SUPPLEMENTARY.
  • Allopurinol/febuxostat + azathioprine can cause severe thiopurine toxicity because xanthine-oxidase metabolism is inhibited.
  • Methotrexate and mycophenolate are contraindicated in pregnancy; hydroxychloroquine is commonly continued in pregnancy when appropriate SUPPLEMENTARY.
  • Monitor disease-specific organs plus CBC, LFTs, creatinine, urinalysis/proteinuria, retinal health and infection.

🩹Psoriasis / Psoriatic Inflammatory Disease

Pathophysiologic Basis
  • The TOS explicitly lists psoriasis under connective-tissue diseases even though it is principally an immune-mediated dermatologic disease.
  • Psoriasis reflects pathologic T-cell/cytokine signaling with accelerated keratinocyte proliferation and inflammatory plaque formation; systemic joint disease represents psoriatic arthritis.
  • Pharmacotherapy can act locally on epidermal proliferation/inflammation or systemically on T cells and cytokines such as TNF, IL-12/23, IL-17 and IL-23 SUPPLEMENTARY.
  • Methotrexate/cyclosporine broadly suppress immune proliferation; biologics interrupt specific cytokine pathways.
  • Pain-only treatment does not alter destructive psoriatic arthritis; disease-modifying therapy is required when inflammatory joint disease is present.
Clinical Features and Diagnostic Strategy
  • Plaque psoriasis produces sharply demarcated erythematous plaques with scale, commonly on extensor surfaces/scalp.
  • Ask about inflammatory back pain, swollen joints, dactylitis and enthesitis to identify psoriatic arthritis.
  • Determine body-surface involvement, special-site involvement, quality-of-life effect and joint disease before selecting topical versus systemic therapy.
  • Before systemic immunosuppression/biologics, evaluate CBC/LFT/renal function, TB/hepatitis risk and active infection SUPPLEMENTARY.
  • Pregnancy, liver disease, renal disease and infection status substantially modify systemic-drug selection.
Pharmacologic Management
  • Topical corticosteroid SUPPLEMENTARY: GR agonism suppresses local inflammatory transcription; prolonged/high-potency use can cause skin atrophy, telangiectasia and systemic HPA suppression when large areas are treated.
  • Vitamin-D analog — Calcipotriene SUPPLEMENTARY: VDR activation normalizes keratinocyte differentiation/proliferation; hypercalcemia is uncommon at standard topical exposure.
  • Methotrexate: cytotoxic/immune-modifying DMARD; used systemically for severe skin disease or psoriatic arthritis; monitor marrow, liver and pregnancy risk.
  • DCyclosporine: calcineurin inhibition → ↓IL-2/T-cell activation; rapid systemic efficacy but nephrotoxicity and hypertension restrict long-term use.
  • TNF inhibitors — Infliximab/Adalimumab/Etanercept: neutralize TNF signaling; useful in inflammatory disease but increase serious infection/TB-reactivation risk.
  • IL-17/IL-23 pathway biologics SUPPLEMENTARY: highly targeted modern options for plaque psoriasis/psoriatic arthritis; infection and agent-specific immune effects require monitoring.
Therapeutic Pitfalls, Contraindications, and Monitoring
  • Chronic systemic glucocorticoid monotherapy is generally undesirable in psoriasis SUPPLEMENTARY because of toxicity and risk of disease rebound.
  • Methotrexate is contraindicated in pregnancy and requires CBC/LFT/creatinine monitoring.
  • Cyclosporine + nephrotoxic drugs or strong CYP3A4 inhibitors can markedly increase renal/systemic toxicity.
  • TNF inhibitors should not be initiated in uncontrolled serious infection and require latent-TB screening.
  • Monitor skin/joint response, CBC, LFTs, renal function, BP with cyclosporine and biologic-specific infection risks.

🦴 Cutaneous & Musculoskeletal System
  • NSAID dose-response: increasing COX inhibition improves inflammatory pain until analgesic ceiling is approached; further dose escalation disproportionately increases GI, renal and BP toxicity rather than producing unlimited analgesia.
  • COX selectivity graph: as selectivity shifts from nonselective NSAIDs toward COX-2, platelet/GI COX-1 inhibition decreases, but renal prostaglandin effects and cardiovascular thrombotic risk are not eliminated.
  • Acetaminophen concentration–time/toxicity graph: normal doses are cleared predominantly through conjugation; overdose saturates safe pathways and progressively increases toxic P450-derived metabolite formation until glutathione is depleted; NAC is most effective when given early.
  • Opioid agonist dose-response: increasing μ-receptor occupancy increases analgesia but also progressively increases respiratory depression, sedation and constipation; high-efficacy full agonists have no reliable respiratory “ceiling.”
  • Competitive antagonist graph: naloxone competitively shifts opioid agonist receptor signaling downward/rightward while present; as naloxone concentration falls, a longer-lasting opioid can reoccupy receptors and cause recurrent toxicity.
  • Bisphosphonate plasma-versus-bone curve: plasma exposure after oral therapy is low, but drug incorporated into bone persists for prolonged periods, so pharmacodynamic skeletal effects far outlast circulating drug.
  • Teriparatide exposure graph: intermittent PTH receptor pulses favor osteoblast-mediated formation, whereas continuous PTH exposure favors net resorption.
  • Urate-lowering curve: effective allopurinol/febuxostat progressively lowers serum urate; early mobilization of tissue urate may transiently increase flare frequency before crystal burden declines.

Key Pharmacologic Points
  • DNSAIDs inhibit COX → ↓prostaglandins → analgesic + antipyretic + anti-inflammatory effects.
  • Aspirin uniquely irreversibly inhibits COX; most other NSAIDs are reversible inhibitors.
  • Celecoxib = COX-2 selective; less platelet/GI COX-1 effect does NOT mean no renal/CV toxicity.
  • Ibuprofen t½ ~2 h; naproxen ~14 h; celecoxib ~11 h in the source.
  • Acetaminophen = analgesic/antipyretic with little anti-inflammatory activity.
  • DAcetaminophen overdose → toxic P450 metabolite → hepatic necrosis → N-acetylcysteine.
  • Morphine = full μ agonist; naloxone = opioid antagonist.
  • DOpioid overdose = CNS depression + respiratory depression + miosis → naloxone.
  • Bisphosphonate oral bioavailability <10%; food makes it worse; esophageal irritation is classic.
  • DDenosumab = anti-RANKL → ↓osteoclast formation/activity.
  • DTeriparatide = intermittent PTH → bone formation.
  • NSAIDs do not modify RA structural disease.
  • Methotrexate = classic anchor DMARD; weekly dosing + CBC/LFT/renal monitoring + pregnancy contraindication.
  • Hydroxychloroquine = retinal toxicity.
  • Leflunomide = hepatotoxic + teratogenic.
  • TNF inhibitors = serious infection + latent-TB reactivation risk.
  • Vancomycin = D-Ala-D-Ala cell-wall drug + renal elimination + nephrotoxicity.
  • Clindamycin = 50S inhibition + C. difficile association.
  • DINH = mycolic-acid inhibition + neuropathy/hepatitis → pyridoxine.
  • Rifampin = RNA-polymerase inhibitor + enzyme induction + orange secretions.
  • Ethambutol = optic neuritis/red-green loss.
  • Pyrazinamide = hyperuricemia/arthralgia + hepatotoxicity.
  • Colchicine = tubulin/microtubule inhibition + diarrhea; it does NOT lower urate.
  • Allopurinol/febuxostat = xanthine-oxidase inhibitors.
  • Allopurinol + azathioprine/6-MP = dangerous interaction.
  • Probenecid = uricosuric proximal-tubule transport effect.
  • Epinephrine is the drug of choice for anaphylaxis; antihistamines/steroids are adjuncts.
  • Sulfonamides are a classic SJS-associated drug class.
  • DCyclophosphamide → acrolein → hemorrhagic cystitis → MESNA + hydration SUPPLEMENTARY.
  • SJS/TEN has no single universal pharmacologic antidote in the supplied source; immediate drug withdrawal is mandatory.
